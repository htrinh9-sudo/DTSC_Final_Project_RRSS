# Recipe Recommender

A content-based recipe recommendation system. You tell it what's in your kitchen, how much time you have, and what you're in the mood for; it ranks recipes by how well they match.

Built as a final project for DTSC coursework at UNC Charlotte.

## How it works

Recipes are represented as TF-IDF vectors over their ingredient text, and user input is vectorized against the same vocabulary. Ranking is cosine similarity between the two.

TF-IDF is the right fit here because it automatically downweights ingredients that appear in nearly every recipe. Salt and water carry almost no signal; miso and plantain carry a lot.

**Inputs:** available ingredients, priority ingredients (typically your protein), max prep time, max cook time, cuisine, and a minimum match threshold.

**Scoring:** 80% ingredient match + 20% recipe rating. Priority ingredients are repeated in the query vector to boost their weight (default multiplier 5.0). Recipes below the match threshold are filtered out before ranking.

**Vectorizer config:** unigrams and bigrams (so "olive oil" and "black beans" survive as units), `min_df=2`, `max_df=0.95`, English stop words removed.

## Experiments

**1 — Baseline.** Precision measured against a rule-based relevance definition: a recipe counts as relevant if it clears the match threshold, contains the priority ingredient, and includes at least two other available ingredients.

**2 — Feature ablation.** Each component is disabled one at a time — rating weight, prep time filter, cook time filter, priority boosting — to isolate what each contributes to precision.

**3 — Binary classification.** The problem is reframed as supervised classification. Recipes are labeled Recommended if they match ≥30% of available ingredients within the time budget. Logistic Regression and Multinomial Naive Bayes are trained on the TF-IDF matrix, with SMOTE applied to the training split only to address class imbalance.

## What I'd change

**The evaluation is circular.** This is the main flaw and it's worth being explicit about. "Relevance" is defined using the same ingredient match score that the recommender ranks on, so precision is measured against the system's own scoring function rather than against what a person would actually want to cook. High precision here is close to guaranteed and doesn't demonstrate much.

The same problem runs through Experiment 3: labels are generated by a rule, then a classifier is trained to reproduce that rule. Strong metrics mean the model learned the labeling function, not anything about food. SMOTE on top of synthetic labels compounds it rather than fixing it.

I also lowered the labeling threshold from 0.60 to 0.30 to generate more positive examples. That makes the classifier trainable, but adjusting the target definition to improve results is exactly the move that should raise a flag.

**Fixing it** requires ground truth independent of the scoring function — human relevance judgments on a sample of query/result pairs, or held-out user behavior. Without that, this is a working retrieval system with an unvalidated ranking, which is a fair thing for it to be as long as it's labeled honestly.

The ablation study is the part I'd keep unchanged. Removing one component at a time to measure its contribution is the right instinct even when the outcome metric is imperfect.
